Above all, we do a lot of cooking in our household. My husband calls the kitchen the engine of the house, and we both firmly believe there should always be a pot of something on the stove, or at least within reach in the fridge, ready to share with anyone stopping by. We love it when neighbors, friends, and family let us know they’ll be in the area and want to swing through.

At the heart of many of the recipes we make (and by "we," I mean Paul) is a really strong stock. Our dear friend Jaylin once said that a stock is only good enough if you can punch it when it’s cooled and it still stands up. Of course, what this means is that it’s full of good collagen and structure from the different parts of a slow-cooked bird or beast. We’ve specialized in two kinds of stock: pork, from the pigs we help slaughter at Sweetwater Hill in Westhampton every fall, and chicken, which we source locally, though we hope to venture into raising meat birds this coming year. This fall, we’ve also been leaning into herbal Chinese soups.

Perhaps more abstractly, I often think about whether what we’re doing at our homestead truly stands up to the words we use to describe it. Are we genuinely embodying social justice? Are we actually meeting the needs of our community? And how are our homesteading practices nourishing both our physical bodies and our hearts?

I’d like to unpack each of these questions in greater detail, though I can already say we’re excited to build out a cohort of families from a range of backgrounds in 2027. Our goal is to deeply understand their needs when it comes to connecting with gardening or homesteading, and to act as a resource that helps them engage with these practices in a meaningful and feasible way.

As our good friend Gaby, a leader at Grow Food in Northampton, has outlined, there are several other ingredients required to succeed in this space: financial support, time, knowledge, and a physical space to garden.

Where I really aim to lean into social justice is by making these essential ingredients available to our 2027 cohort. We aren't imagining that everyone needs a full-blown operation. It could be as simple as a couple of raised beds that provide fresh herbs, or even just flowers to spark a family's interest. Simply enjoying the process of sowing seeds and watching something grow is inherently fruitful.

Among my son’s first words was a lexicon of berries and plants, though not always exact: Husk Cherry became "Hup Chee," Blackberry became "Bap," Blueberry became "Bou Bou," and somehow Watermelon became "Pommeau."

Gardening is as much about passing traditions from generation to generation as it is about the literal harvest.

We share all of this to show how the broth of our vision and tangible next actions holds up. As always, we welcome your feedback. Please reach out if you are interested in applying to our 2027 postage-stamp homestead cohort, and keep your eyes peeled for an application link in November.
