Above all, we do a lot of cooking in our household. My husband calls the kitchen the engine of the house. We both firmly believe that there should be a pot of something on the stove at all times, or at least within reach in the fridge, to share with somebody who’s stopping by. We love when our neighbors, friends, and families let us know that they’ll be in the area and wish to swing by.

At the heart of many of the recipes we make, and by we I mean Paul, is a really strong stock. Our dear friend Jaylin said that a stock is only good enough if you can punch it and it will still stand up when it’s cooled. Of course, what this means is it’s full of good collagen and structure from the different parts of a slow-cooked bird or beast. We’ve specialized in two kinds of stock: pork, from the pigs we help slaughter in West Hampton every fall, and chicken, which we source locally, although we hope to venture into meat birds this coming year. This fall we’ve also been leaning into herbal Chinese soups.

Perhaps more abstractly, I think about whether or not what we’re doing at our homestead really stands up to the words we use to speak about it. Are we really embodying social justice? Are we really meeting the needs of our community? And where are the practices of homesteading for us nourishing both our physical bodies and also our hearts?

I’d like to unpack each one of these investigations in greater detail, although I can say we’re really excited to build out a cohort of other families from a range of backgrounds in 2027 to deeply understand their needs for connecting with gardening or homesteading practices, and to share our knowledge and be a resource to help them engage with homesteading in a meaningful and also feasible way. In addition, as our good friend Gaby, a leader at Grow Food in Northampton, has outlined, there are other ingredients you need to be successful in the homesteading space, such as financial support, time, knowledge, and a physical space to garden.

Where I really aim to lean into practices of social justice is to make these ingredients of homesteading available to a cohort of people from a variety of backgrounds in 2027. We’re not thinking it needs to be a full-blown operation. It could just be a couple of raised beds that provide herbs to families, or maybe just flowers to engage people in the family. Just enjoying the process of sowing seeds and watching something grow is fruitful inherently. Among my son’s first words was a lexicon of berries and plants, not always exact: Husk Cherry = Hup Chee, Blackberry = Bap, Blueberry = Bou Bou, and somehow Watermelon is Pommeau? Gardening is as much the passing of traditions from generation to generation as it is about strictly what is grown.

So we hope to share the above as a way to express how the broth of our vision and tangible next actions holds up.

As always, we welcome feedback. Please reach out if you are interested in applying to our 2027 postage stamp homestead cohort.

Keep your eyes peeled for an application link in November.
